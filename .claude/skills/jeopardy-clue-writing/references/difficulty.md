## The fame ladder

Difficulty is a property of **the answer**, not the sentence. In the real corpus the
clue text stays almost exactly the same size from the cheapest row to the dearest
(14.1 → 15.0 words), while the answer gets steadily less famous: the median $200
answer has been used 18 times across 42 seasons, the median $1000 answer 7 times,
and the share of never-repeated answers doubles from 9% to 18%.

So the question to ask about a draft clue is not "is this hard to read?" but:

> Of the people who will actually be in the room, what fraction could name this
> thing if I described it perfectly?

That fraction *is* the tier:

| fraction who could name it | tier | role in the game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nearly all | row 1 | opens the category, gets everyone buzzing |
| most | row 2 | still comfortable, establishes the theme |
| about half | row 3 | the honest middle |
| a minority | row 4 | rewards someone who knows the subject |
| one person, or nobody | row 5 | the trophy clue |

A board where every clue is row-5 hard is not a hard game, it is a silent one.
The low rows are what make the room noisy, and the noise is the point.

## One fact, five difficulties

Same subject — Santa's reindeer — walked from famous to obscure. Notice the clues
stay the same length and shape; only the target moves.

| tier | clue | response | why it sits here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | This red-nosed reindeer leads the sleigh in fog | Rudolph | known to essentially everyone |
| 2 | This reindeer's name means "one who dashes"; he's first in the poem's list | Dasher | familiar list, first item |
| 3 | This reindeer shares his name with a German word for lightning | Blitzen | needs the list plus one fact |
| 4 | Rudolph's 1964 special gave this coach of the Reindeer Games a stern voice | Comet | deep in a familiar story |
| 5 | In the 1823 poem, Donner and Blitzen first appeared under these two Dutch-ish names | Dunder and Blixem | one person in the room, if that |

The same walk works for any subject: lead → supporting character → the thing next to
the thing → the specific year → the version before the famous version.

## Techniques that raise difficulty honestly

- **Move down the fame list.** The second-most-famous member of a set is a whole
  tier harder than the first, and costs no extra words.
- **Ask for the specific instead of the general.** "This decade" → "this year".
  "This country" → "this city".
- **Ask from the far side.** Instead of describing a person and asking for the name,
  describe the work and ask which person made it.
- **Require the join of two ordinary facts.** Each half is common knowledge; holding
  both at once is the difficulty. This is how Before & After categories work.
- **Use a less-worn attribute.** Everyone knows what a flamingo looks like; fewer
  know why it is pink.

## Techniques that only feel harder

These raise frustration, not difficulty, and real clues avoid them:

- **Padding.** Extra clauses make the clue slower to read, not harder to answer.
- **Ambiguity.** If two answers are defensible, the clue is broken, not difficult.
  A player who says a right-but-unintended answer will remember it all night.
- **Trick phrasing.** Double negatives, deliberately misleading grammar — the game
  is about knowing things, not about parsing.
- **Obscurity with no handhold.** An answer nobody could reason toward is a dead
  clue. Even the hardest clue should leave the room saying "oh, of course" when the
  answer lands — that recognition is what makes hard clues fun.
- **Vocabulary as a gatekeeper.** Using rarer words to describe an easy fact tests
  reading, not knowledge, and it hits kids and non-native speakers hardest.

## Audience calibration

The form stays the same across audiences; the pool of famous things and the
vocabulary ceiling change. In real Kids/Teen tournaments the clues are about a word
shorter than adult ones and name a year noticeably less often (17% vs 22%) — the
convention travels, the content narrows.

| audience | "famous" means | vocabulary ceiling | good subject matter | traps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **kids_4_under** | things they can see, touch, or point at in a picture book | ~8 words, all everyday; no clause stacking | colours, animals, counting to ten, body parts, weather, familiar songs and characters | anything needing reading, spelling, dates, or numbers above ten; abstractions like "recommended" or "protection" |
| **kids_10_under** | school subjects and the shows, games and books they actually consume | short sentences, concrete nouns; a hard word is fine if the clue explains it | animals and habitats, planets, simple history and geography, holidays, sports, cartoons | teen/adult media; anything from before their parents were young; wordplay needing spelling knowledge |
| **teenagers** | current music, film, games and internet culture, plus what school has covered | adult vocabulary, but keep the sentence one clause | recent releases, memes with staying power, science and history from the syllabus, sport | "when I was your age" references; anything assuming adult life admin |
| **adults** | general knowledge — the real Jeopardy target | full adult range | history, geography, literature, science, film, food, sport, current affairs | in-group jargon; assuming a specialty everyone shares |
| **no_humanity** | the crude, rude, adults-after-dark set | adult range, plus the rude register the theme promises | the risqué and the gross, played for laughs | cruelty aimed at real people or groups; explicitness with no joke in it — it should still be a *game*, and it still needs a single defensible answer |

The most common failure by far is writing an adult clue and lowering the number on
it. If a "kids" clue would be a perfectly ordinary adult clue, it is not calibrated —
it is mislabelled. This is worth checking deliberately, because it is invisible from
inside the writing: look at the *answer* and ask whether a child of that age has
ever encountered the thing at all.

## Value ladders per audience

This project uses five values per category and a different ladder per audience —
smaller numbers for the youngest players:

| audience | values |
| --- | --- |
| kids_4_under | 100, 100, 200, 200, 300 |
| kids_10_under | 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 |
| teenagers | 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 |
| adults | 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 |
| no_humanity | 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 |

The numbers matter less than the spread: whatever the ladder, row 1 should be a
gift and row 5 should be earned, *within that audience's world*. A $300 clue for
four-year-olds and a $1000 clue for adults are both "the hardest one here", and
both should still be answerable by someone in the room.

## Testing a tier you are unsure about

Three quick checks, in order of usefulness:

1. **Name three people who would get it.** If you can picture the specific person in
   the family who knows this, you have the tier: the youngest child (row 1), most of
   the table (row 2–3), the one uncle who follows the subject (row 4–5).
2. **Compare within the category, not against the world.** Put the five answers in a
   column and sort them by fame. If two of them tie, one needs replacing — a ladder
   with a flat step plays as a mistake.
3. **Check the answer is reachable.** Read only the clue and try to reason toward
   the answer without knowing it. If there is no path at all, add a handhold (a
   date, a shared attribute, a rhyme) rather than lowering the tier.
