## Inverting a quiz question into a clue

Most trivia arrives as a question. Converting it is mechanical once seen:

| quiz question | clue | response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What number SPF is recommended for a day at the beach? | Dermatologists say this SPF number or higher for a day at the beach | What is 30? |
| Which planet is closest to the Sun? | This planet takes just 88 days to orbit the Sun, the shortest year of any | What is Mercury? |
| Who wrote *Romeo and Juliet*? | This playwright killed off a pair of "star-cross'd lovers" from Verona | Who is Shakespeare? |

The move is always the same: take the interrogative, turn it into a statement about
the thing, and replace the name of the thing with **this/these** plus its category
noun. If the resulting sentence still reads like a question, the pointer is probably
missing.

Note the second and third examples gained a fact in the conversion ("88 days",
"Verona"). That is the other half of the job: a bare inversion — "This planet is
closest to the Sun" — is correct but flat. Real clues almost always carry one
specific detail, because the detail is what makes the answer feel earned.

## Pointer words and the response

83% of real clues contain *this*, *these*, *his*, *her* or *it*. The pointer is
doing grammatical work: it tells the player what kind of thing to name, which
determines the question word they use.

| pointer | response opens | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| this person / this author / this president | Who is… | Who is Lincoln? |
| this city / this novel / this element | What is… | What is Neon? |
| these birds / these two brothers | What are / Who are… | What are penguins? |

The player is not marked down for the wrong question word in casual play, so do not
agonise — but pick the pointer that matches the answer's number and animacy, because
a mismatch ("this composer" → answer is a duo) reads as a mistake.

Clues that are just a term with no sentence around it are legitimate and common in
short-form categories — "Iron pyrite" → *What is fool's gold?* — especially where the
category title supplies the frame.

## What a response may be

Real responses average 1.8 words. A response should be a **name, title, place,
number, or thing**:

- Good: `Mercury`, `Charles Dickens`, `the Jordan`, `30`, `a MiG`, `Beverly Hills, 90210`
- Not a response: `Chemical absorbers prevent photochemical damage; physical
  blockers prevent collagen breakdown` — that is a lecture. Nobody can say it at a
  buzzer, and no host can rule on it.

If the only true answer to a clue is a sentence, the clue is asking the wrong
question. Find the noun inside it and ask for that instead: *"This mineral-based
sunscreen ingredient sits on top of the skin and reflects UV rather than absorbing
it"* → *What is zinc oxide?*

Keep articles natural (`the Jordan`, `a MiG`) and let near-misses count — a player
saying "Dickens" for "Charles Dickens" is right.

## Category naming

Measured across 48,133 distinct real categories: 96.5% are ALL CAPS, the median
title is 3 words, 10.3% contain quotation marks, and 4.6% use an ampersand. The most
reused titles are `BEFORE & AFTER`, `SCIENCE`, `POTPOURRI`, `AMERICAN HISTORY`,
`WORD ORIGINS`, `RHYME TIME`.

Good titles are short, concrete, and promise a shape: `BEACH BIRDS`,
`HOLIDAY BAKING`, `NEW YEAR AROUND THE WORLD`. Avoid titles that only make sense
after you have read the clues, and avoid titles so broad ("MISCELLANEOUS") that the
player gets no help — the title is the player's first handhold on every clue beneath
it.

Titles like `BIRTHDAY AGE NUMBERS - COUNTING YEARS` are doing two jobs and should be
one: `COUNTING THE YEARS`.

## Wordplay categories

The quoted-title convention signals a constraint every answer obeys:

- `"FOOL" HOUSE` — every answer contains *fool* (fool's gold, The Fool on the Hill).
- `RHYME TIME` — the answer is two rhyming words (a *funny bunny*).
- `BEFORE & AFTER` — two clues joined at a shared word (*Santa Claus von Bülow*).

These are the most fun categories to play and the fiddliest to write, because the
constraint has to hold across all five difficulty steps. Write the constraint first,
brainstorm every answer that satisfies it, *then* sort by fame and pick five. Trying
to force a constraint onto an already-chosen set of answers rarely works.

Put the gimmick in the title's quotation marks rather than explaining it in the
clues — players enjoy spotting it, and the discovery is part of the category.

## Failure modes, with fixes

**The answer is in the clue.** *"This Christmas tree ornament tops the Christmas
tree"* → the player just repeats the words back. Fix by describing the thing without
its own name: *"It goes on last, at the very top, often a star or an angel."* Watch
for shared roots too — "sunscreen" leaks "sun".

**Two defensible answers.** *"This Beatle wrote 'Yesterday'"* is fine;
*"This Beatle sang lead on many hits"* is not. Add the detail that excludes the
rivals, or ask about something singular in the first place.

**The clue asks for a list.** *"Name Santa's reindeer"* has eight answers and no
buzzer moment. Ask for one: *"This reindeer's name means thunder in German."*

**Dates used as decoration.** A year in the clue should help identify the answer, not
merely date it. Roughly a fifth of real clues name a year — always as a
discriminator.

**Vocabulary above the audience.** *"What number SPF sunscreen is commonly
recommended for beach protection?"* aimed at four-year-olds fails twice: the
subject is abstract and the words are adult. The fix is not simplification of the
sentence but a different answer entirely — *"You put on this white cream so the sun
doesn't hurt your skin"* → *What is sunscreen?*

**Category drift.** A clue that would sit equally well in any category weakens the
board. If the category is `BEACH BIRDS`, the answer should be a bird, and knowing
that should help.

**Rudeness without a joke.** In an adults-only category, the crude answer still has
to be an *answer* — something the room can name and laugh at. Shock with no target
to guess is not a clue.
